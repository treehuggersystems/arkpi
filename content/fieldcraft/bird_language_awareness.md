# Bird Language & Awareness

Long before you see a predator, a person, or a deer, the birds and squirrels have already announced it. Learning to listen is old knowledge that hunters, scouts and tribes have always used.

## The Five Voices of Birds
| Voice | Sounds like | Means |
|---|---|---|
| **Song** | Long, musical, repeated (cardinals, robins, wrens in spring) | All is well: territory and mating |
| **Companion calls** | Soft chips and contact notes between pairs/flocks | Relaxed |
| **Juvenile begging** | Insistent, repetitive squeaks | Relaxed |
| **Aggression** | Squabbling between birds | Normal competition |
| **Alarm** | Sharp, harsh, repeated calls; **sudden silence** | **Something is moving that shouldn't be** |

**The baseline matters most.** When birds sing and chip normally, the area is calm. When song stops or alarms go up, something has changed.

## Reading Alarms
- **Sudden silence spreading in a wave** ahead of something moving: a "bird plow." Often a person walking or a predator. You can follow its path by the quiet.
- **Robins and cardinals:** sharp repeated "tuk tuk" or "chip chip" when alarmed.
- **Blue jays:** loud "jay! jay!" alarm; also mimic hawk calls.
- **Crows mobbing:** a noisy group of crows diving and calling at one spot usually means an owl or hawk.
- **Chickadees and titmice:** more "dee-dee-dee" notes at the end of the call means more danger. They mob small owls.
- **Squirrels:** chattering and tail flicking from a branch, staring in one direction = they see a threat. Follow their gaze.
- **Birds flushing low and fast into cover** = hawk overhead. **Birds flushing up into trees** = ground predator or person.
- **Wrens, towhees and sparrows** scolding from low brush = cat, fox, snake, or person on the ground.
- **Deer snorting/blowing** and stamping = they smell or see you.

## Sit Spot: The Core Practice
Pick a spot near home (a tree line, a creek edge, the backyard). Sit there quietly for **20 minutes, often**. Same place, different times and seasons.
- The first 10–15 minutes, the birds "settle" from your arrival. Then the woods come back to life.
- You'll learn the baseline: which birds live there, their routines, what's normal.
- It trains patience, hearing and the ability to notice change. That pays off in hunting, security and peace of mind.

## Wide-Angle Vision ("Owl Eyes")
Your eyes default to tunnel focus. Peripheral vision catches **movement** far better.
1. Hold your arms out to the sides and wiggle your fingers.
2. Look straight ahead, soften your gaze, and bring your arms back until you just see your fingers moving at both edges.
3. Keep that wide, soft awareness as you walk. Movement jumps out, especially at dawn and dusk.

## Moving Quietly ("Fox Walking")
- Slow down. Shorter steps, knees slightly bent.
- Land on the **outside edge of the ball of your foot**, feel for sticks, then roll down and shift weight.
- Stop often to look and listen. Animals move in bursts too.
- Move when the wind blows or a stream masks sound.
- Stay off the skyline (ridge tops), use shadows, keep the wind in your face so your scent goes behind you.

## Using It
- **Hunting:** alarmed birds and squirrels often mark where deer or turkey are moving, and tell you when you've been spotted.
- **Security:** a sudden silence or alarm chorus near your home at night or dawn is worth checking.
- **Kids:** sit-spot and "bird alarm" games teach patience and awareness, and they're fun.
