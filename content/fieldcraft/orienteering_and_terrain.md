# Orienteering, Terrain & Estimation

The basics of map and compass are in [Map & Compass Navigation](/navigation/map_and_compass). This is the next level: moving fast and confidently across real terrain, and estimating things without tools.

## Orienteering Techniques
- **Orient the map** constantly: turn it so north on the map points north on the ground (by compass or by matching features). Then left on the map is left in front of you.
- **Thumb the map:** keep your thumb on your current position and slide it as you move. You'll never need to search the whole map to find yourself.
- **Simplify:** break a long route into legs between big, obvious features.
- **Handrails:** follow linear features (stream, power line, fence, ridge, trail) that run your direction.
- **Collecting features:** things you'll pass that confirm you're on track ("cross a creek, then a fence, then the trail").
- **Catching feature:** something big beyond your destination that tells you you've gone too far (a road, river, field edge).
- **Attack point:** an obvious feature near a hard-to-find target. Navigate fast to the attack point, then go carefully by compass and pace count for the last short stretch.
- **Aiming off:** to hit a point on a linear feature (a bridge on a creek), aim deliberately to one side. When you reach the creek, you know which way to turn.
- **Route choice:** the straight line isn't always fastest. Going around a hill on a trail often beats going over it. Compare climb, thickness of brush, and how easy navigation will be.

## Travel Time: Naismith's Rule
Plan **1 hour for every 3 miles (5 km)** on flat ground, **plus 1 hour for every 2,000 ft (600 m) of climbing**. Add time for brush, snow, loads, darkness, and tired people. Descents over steep ground are slower than you think.

## When You're Lost: Relocation
1. **STOP:** Sit, Think, Observe, Plan. Don't keep walking blindly.
2. Note where you **last knew** your position and roughly how long and which direction you've traveled since.
3. Look for big features: which way is downhill? Any water, ridgelines, roads, power lines, noises (traffic, trains)?
4. **Resection** with two or three known landmarks if you can see them (see [Map & Compass](/navigation/map_and_compass)).
5. If unsure, walk on a fixed bearing to a big catching feature (a road or river you know is in that direction).
6. In real trouble, especially if someone knows your plan: **stay put**, make shelter, and signal.

## Estimating Distance by Eye
| You can see… | Roughly |
|---|---|
| Individual eyes clearly | 50 yards |
| Eyes as dots, clothing details | 100 yards |
| Face features fade, clothing colors clear | 200 yards |
| A person's head as a dot, limbs visible | 300–400 yards |
| A person as a post, no limbs | 600+ yards |
| Windows on a house | ~1 mile |
| Large trees as individual trees | ~2 miles |

Things look **closer** in bright light, clear air, across water or snow, or looking up/downhill. Things look **farther** in haze, dusk, or with only the top showing.

**Football-field method:** picture how many 100-yard football fields fit between you and the object.

## The Thumb Method (distance to an object of known width)
1. Hold your arm straight out, thumb up. Close one eye and line your thumb up with one side of the object.
2. Switch eyes without moving your thumb: it seems to jump sideways.
3. Estimate how far it jumped **in real-world terms** (for example, "the width of 3 cars ≈ 15 yards").
4. Multiply that by **10**: about 150 yards away. (Your arm is about 10× the distance between your eyes.)

## Estimating Height
**Stick method:** hold a stick vertically at arm's length so the part above your fist equals the length of your arm. Walk until the stick covers the tree exactly from base to top. The distance from you to the tree is about its height. Or use the same shadow ratio: height = (your height ÷ your shadow length) × the tree's shadow length.

## Time Until Sunset (the hand method)
Hold your arm straight out, hand flat and sideways, fingers together, with your bottom finger on the horizon. Each finger-width between the horizon and the sun is about **15 minutes** of daylight left (a full hand ≈ 1 hour). Works best at mid-latitudes; less accurate in summer and winter extremes. Get exact times from the [Sun & Moon tool](/tools/sun).

## Night Movement
- Let eyes adapt **20–30 minutes**; use **red light** only (see ARK·π's red night mode).
- Look slightly to the side of an object: night vision is better off-center.
- Navigate by the stars (see [Navigating by the Stars](/fieldcraft/stars_navigation)) and big terrain shapes against the sky.
- Pace count matters more at night; everything feels farther.
