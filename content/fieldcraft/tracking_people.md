# Tracking People

The most important use of human tracking is **finding someone who's lost**: a child who wandered off, an injured hunter, a confused elderly neighbor. It's also how you know who has been through your property.

## Start Right
1. **Protect the starting point** (the last known location). Keep everyone else from walking over it.
2. **Find the first clear print** and study it: tread pattern, size, any wear or cuts in the sole. Sketch or photograph it with something for scale.
3. **Measure the stride**: heel of one print to heel of the next. Walking adults are around 26–30 in; children much less. A longer stride means running or long legs.

## The Tracking Stick
A straight stick or trekking pole is your best tool.
1. Lay it beside one print. Mark the stick (rubber bands or notches) at the **heel** of one print and the heel of the next: that's the stride.
2. Mark the length of the print too.
3. When you lose the trail, put the back mark at the last print. The next print should be **near the tip** of the stick. Sweep the tip in a small arc and search closely there.

## What to Look For
| Sign | Description |
|---|---|
| **Flattening** | Bent or crushed grass, leaves pressed flat |
| **Color change** | Bruised grass looks darker or shinier; overturned leaves and rocks show a darker, damp underside |
| **Disturbed dew / frost** | A dark line through a dewy or frosted field: very fresh |
| **Transfer** | Mud carried onto rocks, logs, or pavement; water splashed out of puddles |
| **Breaks** | Snapped twigs (fresh breaks are light colored inside), broken spider webs at face height |
| **Scuffs** | Moss scraped off logs or rocks, kicked-up soil |
| **Litter & items** | Wrappers, cloth threads on thorns, dropped gear |
| **Direction** | Bent grass points the way of travel; soil pushed back from the toe |

## Sign-Cutting
When the trail is lost or you need to find where someone left an area:
- Walk a big **circle or line** across their likely path (around a camp, along a road or creek) looking for any sign that crosses it.
- Roads, trail edges, creek banks, fence lines and soft ground are natural "cut" lines where any crossing shows.
- **Aging:** compare with a print you make yourself. Fresh = sharp edges; hours old = edges crumbling, dew on top, debris in the print.

## Lost-Person Behavior: Where to Look
| Who | Tends to… |
|---|---|
| Young children (1–6) | Not travel far (often under 1–2 miles), hide or fall asleep in sheltered spots, not answer calls (fear, "stranger danger"). Search **close** and check small spaces |
| Older children | Travel farther, follow trails or try shortcuts, may hide if they think they're in trouble |
| Elderly / dementia | Go straight until stuck in brush or a fence corner; often found near roads or structures; may not respond to their name |
| Hunters, hikers | Follow drainages downhill, or roads and trails |
| Despondent people | Seek scenic or isolated high points |

**Search tips:** call the person's name, then **stay silent and listen**. Use whistles (3 blasts = help). Search at dawn and dusk. Check water hazards first. Ask: what were they wearing, what shoes, any medical needs? Mark searched areas on the [map](/map).

## Tracking at Night
A flashlight held **low to the ground, nearly horizontal**, makes prints throw strong shadows. Many trackers find night tracking easier than midday.

## Being Hard to Track
Walk on rock, roots, leaf litter, or in shallow streams; avoid soft mud and dew-covered grass; don't break branches; travel in single file.
