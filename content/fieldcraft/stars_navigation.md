# Navigating by the Stars

The night sky is a compass and a clock that never needs batteries. With no city lights after a grid-down event, the stars will be brighter than most people alive have ever seen them.

## How the Sky Moves
- The whole sky turns **east to west** around a point near **Polaris**, about **15° per hour** (one fist at arm's length ≈ 10°).
- Stars rise in the east and set in the west, just like the sun.
- The same star rises about **4 minutes earlier** each night, which is why the constellations change with the seasons.

## North: Polaris (the North Star)
**Big Dipper method**
1. Find the **Big Dipper** (seven bright stars: a ladle).
2. Take the two stars at the outer edge of the cup, the **pointers** (Merak and Dubhe).
3. Follow the line out from the cup's bottom through its lip about **5 times** the distance between them.
4. You reach a moderately bright, lonely star: **Polaris**, the end of the Little Dipper's handle. It's within about 1° of **true north**.

**Cassiopeia method** (when the Dipper is low or hidden in fall)
Cassiopeia is a bright **W** (or M) on the opposite side of Polaris from the Big Dipper. Polaris lies off the middle of the W, about the same distance as the W is wide.

**Polaris isn't the brightest star.** It's only medium-bright. Use the pointers; don't guess.

## Your Latitude
The height of Polaris above the horizon equals your **latitude**. In Kentucky and Ohio (37°–41° N) it sits a bit less than halfway up the sky. Measure with fists: one fist at arm's length ≈ 10°, so about 4 fists up.

## East & West: Orion (winter)
- **Orion** dominates winter evenings: three bright stars in a row (the **Belt**) with Betelgeuse (red) and Rigel (blue-white) above and below.
- The Belt **rises almost exactly due east and sets almost due west**, anywhere on Earth.
- Orion's Sword hangs down from the belt and points roughly **south**.

## Other Direction Tricks
- **Any star:** watch a bright star against a fixed point (a stick, a tree branch) for 15–20 minutes.
  - **Rising** → you're facing **east**. **Sinking** → **west**.
  - Moving **right** (with you facing it) → **south**. Moving **left** → **north**.
- **Scorpius** (summer): a big hook-shaped constellation with red Antares, low in the **south** on summer evenings.
- **Summer Triangle** (Vega, Deneb, Altair): high overhead on summer nights; Altair at the bottom points roughly south.

## Holding a Course at Night
1. Find your direction (Polaris, Orion).
2. Pick a star low on the horizon in the direction you want to go, and walk toward it.
3. Stars move about 15° per hour, so **switch to a new guide star every 15–20 minutes**, or check Polaris again.
4. In woods, use the star to pick the next landmark (a tree shape against the sky), walk to it, repeat.

## The Moon
- A crescent's horns point away from the sun. Draw a line through the two horns down to the horizon: that's roughly **south** in the Northern Hemisphere.
- A moon that rises **before sunset** has its lit side toward the **west**; one rising **after midnight** is lit on the **east**.
- The **full moon** rises at sunset in the east and sets at sunrise in the west, and is due south at midnight.

## The Sky by Season (evening, Ohio Valley)
| Season | Look for |
|---|---|
| **Winter** | Orion, Taurus (red Aldebaran, Pleiades cluster), Sirius (brightest star) low southeast, Gemini |
| **Spring** | Big Dipper high overhead. Follow the handle's **arc to Arcturus** (orange) and **speed on to Spica**. Leo's backwards question mark |
| **Summer** | Summer Triangle overhead, Scorpius low south, Milky Way overhead on dark nights |
| **Fall** | Cassiopeia's W high in the north, the Great Square of Pegasus, Andromeda |

**Planets** (Venus, Jupiter, Mars, Saturn) shine steadily without twinkling and wander along the same path as the sun and moon. Don't navigate by them.
