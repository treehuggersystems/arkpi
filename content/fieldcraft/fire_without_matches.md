# Fire Without Matches

Lighters and matches run out. These methods don't. Learn them **now**, in good weather, when you don't need them. See [Fire Starting Basics](/homestead/fire_starting_basics) for building and feeding the fire itself.

## Tinder: The Real Secret
A spark or coal is tiny. It needs fine, **bone-dry** tinder that catches easily.
| Tinder | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Cedar / juniper inner bark** | Rub between your palms into a fluffy nest. Excellent |
| **Dry grass** (dead, standing) | Fine and plentiful; bundle it tightly |
| **Cattail fluff** | Catches sparks instantly, burns fast. Mix with grass |
| **Birch bark** | Burns even damp (oily); shave thin curls |
| **Fatwood** (resin-soaked pine) | Found in old pine stumps and knots; smells like turpentine; scrape fine shavings. Burns wet |
| **Punk wood** (dry rotted wood) | Holds a coal |
| **Char cloth** | Catches a spark from flint and steel |
| **Cotton balls + petroleum jelly** | Best modern tinder; carry it |
| Dryer lint, cotton cloth, fine wood shavings ("feather sticks") | Good backups |

**Tinder bundle:** a bird's-nest ball of fine fibers with a pocket in the middle for the coal, surrounded by slightly coarser material.

## Bow Drill
Friction fire with a bow, spindle and fireboard.

### Parts
| Part | Size & material |
|---|---|
| **Fireboard (hearth)** | Flat board ~1 in thick, 2–3 in wide, a foot long. Dry, soft, non-resinous wood |
| **Spindle (drill)** | Straight, ~¾ in thick, 8–10 in long, rounded at the bottom, pointed at the top |
| **Bearing block (handhold)** | Fist-sized hard wood or a stone with a small socket; lubricate with grease or leaf wax |
| **Bow** | A slightly curved stick about arm length; cord (paracord, bootlace) tied with a little slack |
| **Ember pan** | A leaf or chip of bark under the notch to catch the coal |

**Good woods (Eastern US):** basswood, cottonwood, willow, eastern red cedar, tulip poplar, box elder, sycamore. Spindle and board can be the same wood. Wood must be **dead and dry**. Snap test: it should snap, not bend.

### Steps
1. Carve a small starter depression near the edge of the fireboard. Twist the spindle in it with the bow to burn a round socket.
2. Cut a **V-notch** from the board's edge to the center of the socket (about ⅛ of the circle, like a pie slice). This is where hot dust collects.
3. Put the ember pan under the notch. Kneel with your left foot (if right-handed) on the board next to the socket.
4. Wrap the bow string once around the spindle (spindle on the outside of the string). Put the spindle in the socket and the bearing block on top, with your wrist locked against your shin for stability.
5. Saw the bow back and forth with **long, smooth, full-length strokes**. Start moderate, then speed up and add downward pressure as smoke builds.
6. When there's lots of smoke and the notch is full of **dark brown/black dust**, give 10–20 more fast strokes, then stop gently.
7. If the dust keeps smoking on its own, you have a **coal**. Let it grow for a few seconds; fan it gently.
8. Tip the coal into the tinder bundle, fold the bundle around it, and **blow gently, steadily**, raising it as it smokes heavily, until it bursts into flame.

**Troubleshooting:** squeaking or shiny socket = wood too hard or glazed (roughen with sand or a knife); spindle flying out = hold the bearing block steady against your leg; light tan dust = not enough speed or pressure; no coal = damp wood or the notch is too small.

## Flint & Steel
1. A **high-carbon steel** striker (old file, purpose-made striker) and a **hard, sharp-edged rock**: flint, chert or quartz (Kentucky has plenty of chert).
2. Hold the rock with a sharp edge up and a piece of **char cloth** on top of it, under your thumb.
3. Strike the steel down glancingly across the rock's edge. Hot steel sparks land on the char cloth and make a glowing spot.
4. Put the glowing char cloth in a tinder bundle and blow it to flame.

*A ferrocerium rod ("ferro rod") is different and easier: scrape it hard with the back of a knife and its hot sparks light tinder directly, even when wet. Carry one.*

## Making Char Cloth
1. Cut **100% cotton** cloth (old jeans or T-shirt) into 2-inch squares.
2. Put them in a small metal tin (an Altoids tin) with a **tiny nail hole** in the lid.
3. Set the tin in the coals of a fire. Smoke and flame jet from the hole.
4. When the smoke **stops**, remove the tin and let it cool **closed** (opening it hot lets air in and burns the cloth).
5. Good char cloth is black, flexible and not falling apart. It catches a spark instantly.
*Also works with punk wood, cattail heads, and natural fibers.*

## Other Methods
- **Magnifying lens / reading glasses / clear water bottle / ice lens:** focus the sun to the smallest, brightest point on dark tinder or char cloth. Needs strong sun.
- **Battery + steel wool:** touch both terminals of a 9-volt (or a flashlight battery with wire) to fine steel wool. It ignites instantly. Put it in a tinder bundle.
- **Hand drill:** spinning a straight dry stalk (mullein, goldenrod, cattail stalk) between your palms on a soft board. Harder than a bow drill; needs practice and tough hands.
- **Fire plow:** rubbing a hardwood stick fast along a groove in a soft board. Very hard work.

## Keep Fire Going
- Carry a coal in a **fire horn**: a container with dry punk wood or fungus (true tinder fungus / horse hoof fungus on birch), so a coal smolders for hours.
- Bank the fire at night: bury coals under ash to restart in the morning.
- Store tinder and fatwood dry in a bag or tin.
