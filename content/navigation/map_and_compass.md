# Map & Compass Navigation

The [offline map](/map) shows MGRS/UTM coordinates and bearings, but **print paper maps of your area** — batteries die.

## Parts of a Baseplate Compass
- **Baseplate** with a **direction-of-travel arrow**
- **Rotating bezel (housing)** marked 0–360°
- **Magnetic needle** (red end points to magnetic north)
- **Orienting arrow** and orienting lines inside the housing
- **Index line**, where you read the bearing

## Declination
Magnetic north ≠ true (map) north. The difference is **declination**.
- **Kentucky / Ohio / Indiana: roughly 4–7° WEST** (2020s), shifting slowly. Check the margin of a recent topo map.
- West declination: the needle points **west** of true north.
- **Map → field (true → magnetic):** for west declination **ADD**; for east **SUBTRACT**. Mnemonic: "*East is least, West is best*."
- **Field → map (magnetic → true):** do the opposite.
- Or set adjustable declination on your compass once and forget it.

## Take a Bearing in the Field
1. Point the direction-of-travel arrow at the target.
2. Turn the bezel until the **orienting arrow lines up under the red needle** ("**red in the shed**").
3. Read the number at the index line. That's your magnetic bearing.

## Follow a Bearing
1. Set the bearing at the index line.
2. Hold the compass flat at your chest, turn your **whole body** until red is in the shed.
3. Look along the direction arrow, **pick a landmark** (tree, rock), walk to it, repeat.

## Take a Bearing From the Map
1. Lay the compass edge from your position to the destination (direction arrow pointing toward destination).
2. Rotate the bezel so its orienting lines are **parallel to the map's north-south grid lines**, orienting arrow pointing **map north**.
3. Read the bearing at the index line (true). **Adjust for declination** to get the magnetic bearing to walk.

## Reading Topographic Maps
- **Contour lines** connect points of equal elevation. **Close together = steep**, far apart = gentle.
- **V's point uphill** where they cross streams ("V's point to the source").
- Concentric closed loops = hilltop. Hachured (tick-marked) loops = depression.
- Common contour interval: 10–20 ft on US 1:24,000 topos.
- **1:24,000:** 1 inch = 2,000 ft. **1:50,000:** 2 cm = 1 km. **1:100,000:** 1 cm = 1 km.

## Pace Count
- Count every time your **left foot** hits the ground (a "double step").
- Typical: **60–70 double steps per 100 meters** on flat ground. Calibrate yours on a measured 100 m course.
- Uphill, mud, snow, darkness → more paces. Use **ranger beads** to track hundreds.

## Fix Your Position: Resection
1. Identify **two or three landmarks** you can see and find on the map (peaks, towers, water tanks).
2. Take a magnetic bearing to each. Convert to true, then to the **back bearing** (±180°).
3. Draw each back-bearing line from the landmark on the map. **Where they cross is you.** Three lines make a small triangle; you're inside it.

## Navigation Techniques
- **Handrails:** follow linear features (streams, ridges, power lines, roads).
- **Catching features:** a big feature beyond your target that tells you you've gone too far.
- **Aiming off:** aim deliberately left/right of a target on a linear feature, so you know which way to turn when you hit it.
- **Terrain association:** constantly match what you see to the map. It beats compass-only navigation.

## Grid Coordinates (MGRS)
"**Read right, then up.**" 4 digits = 1 km square · 6 digits = 100 m · 8 digits = 10 m · 10 digits = 1 m. See [MGRS & UTM](/navigation/mgrs_utm_grid).

## Priority
- **Know where you are before you're lost. Trust your compass over your gut.**
